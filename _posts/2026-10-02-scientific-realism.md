# I do not believe that electrons are real

Unfortunately, as a university student, I have to take some silly courses related to "critical thinking" and "argumentation". Moreover, as many other natural science students, I find those courses kind of boring and useless.

This week, as I was doing the reading for one of those courses, the topic at hand was epistemology and how science should be looked at critically. There was a TED TALK about how *"Wrong theories can make correct predictions"* blah blah blah blah... You can tell I care very little for this conversation because I believe it approaches the topic from a completely incorrect point of view. 

Throughout my studies I have interacted with many intelligent people and active researchers and have never heard a single one of them be 100% sure of their theory. Science is not a religion and it cannot be treated as one. Personally, I do not care if a theory or model is "wrong" as long as it can make useful predictions. In highschool chemistry, we still use Bohr's model of an atom, electrons orbitting around a nucleus. Many non-scientists, especially those remotely interested in pop science will know that Bohr's model is wrong. If Bohr's model represented reality, we literally would not exist. It is like my quantum mechanics professor has said "Bohr's model of an atom is wrong and I can prove it to you by just standing here". Yet, we still use it. Sometimes it is really useful to simplify things to absurdity in order to get a general grasp of them. And Bohr's model works to make certain important and *correct* predictions. 

I think most scientists are aware of this. This is why the whole "look at explanations critically" argument does not make sense to me at all. Yes, science is concerned with *explaining* things as much as it is concerned with *predicting* things and I am definitely focusing more on the prediction part. However, what is the point of the explanation? Why do we want to explain things? I think this is because we want to use those things in predictions. Saying "the scientific method is not full-proof and scientists work with their implicit biases" is not very productive. We are going to the area of absurd agnosticism. Focusing on "I cannot *know* everything because I operate within the system of my beliefs and virtues" is a paralyzing idea. It is an idea that will get us nowhere, even if it is true. 

I do not think people are concerned with the truth. The medical consensus for many unnecessary tests and scans is to not do it because you will always find something "wrong" with a person and it may just cause unnecessary worries rather than help their health. Sometimes, that "wrong" will be malignant but sometimes it will be something completely benign that now plagues the person's mind with unknowingness and hopelessness. These "critical thinking" proponents, in my opinion, are stepping on the same rake. They want to look as if they are seeking the ultimate truth when in reality they are just abstracting ideas to the point of intellectual paralysis and stagnation. This is exactly the same criticism I have for postmodernist philosophers. "You are wrong and there is nothing you can do about it"

In reality, scientific models, be it the atomic model or the model for traffic on highways, they never have the goal of representing the "true" reality but rather an idealized reality that comes as close to the real reality as is necessary to make a particular prediction or to (dis)prove a particular hypothesis. Just like Einstein has said: "As far as the laws of mathematics refer to reality, they are not certain; and as far as they are certain, they do not refer to reality." It is, however, elegantily fascinating how close to reality we can get once we make some reasonable simplifications. Of course, the more simplified, the more "wrong" the model is but humanity is yet to invent a computer that would have the means to simulated reality *precisely*. That is the whole point. Science is very much aware of its limitations and it works within them. 

I felt quite unconvinced when I saw Newton's F=ma. I was like, no, such a simple formula, describing basically the whole of motion around us? Of course, this is not the case. This is just a very reasonably close approximation to reality. Now, I am taking a course about condensed matter and we described molecular crystals with an *experimental* (not even purely mathematically derived!) Lennard-Jones potential whose repulsion term is approximated as something proportional to 1/r^12. The professor said: "this term could be ~1/r^24 or it could be an exponential". This is just an experimental approximation! But it allows us to make certain predictions. 

Same with electrons. My dad said he found it fascinating and puzzling that a single photon going though a slit can produce a diffraction pattern and I told him that is because photons are not real. My sister reacted with a very flabbergasted "Whaa?!" And I said "show me a photon? I have never seen one. Same with electrons. I do not think electrons are real. I've never seen even a picture." My mom then said "you can see traces of electrons in a cloud chamber." And I answered: "How? when I walk, my footsteps, my traces, are smaller than I am. So you mean to tell me that an electron is so tiny I cannot see it but I can see its footsteps?" Of course, I was being intentionally silly. But it is true. Photons and electrons are whatever you need them to be. Sometimes they are "packets" of energy (like a pack of chips or what??), sometimes they are waves, sometimes they are just probability amplitudes. Of course, this is my framework of thought. I am very much a scientific anti-realist. You may say "but we use electrons in electron microscopy!" But again, what is this electron we are using? Is it a tiny ball, a wave, a soul? To me, it does not matter as long as it gives me the predictions that I need. 

I understand that my argumentation course is supposed to be for everyone, from business management to computer science students and everyone comes from different schools of thought. Yet, I feel this question of scientific fidelity was so disgustingly oversimplified and not given the very deserved justice. To end, I would like to note that my ideas here are not something I will dogmatically stand by forever. It would defeat the whole purpose of this inquisitive engagement that I try to practice. I know that some of this stuff does not fit neatly with dialectics and materialism and I have a long way to go before I can present my ideas in a more polished manner. But for now, electrons are not real to me. They might help me understand reality but I will believe an electron when I see one.

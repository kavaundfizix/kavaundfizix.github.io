## "She told me she needed some time and space... Is she calculating velocity??

Throughout the course of my studies I have started becoming increasingly more independent in my academic endeavours. It is already not automatically the case that I will struggle while reading a research paper or be particularly challenged by novel concepts. Not unlike many young (and probably old) scientists, I often feel like I can never study enough. I don't remember the last time I picked up a novel to read recreationally when there are so many non-fiction books to be read. However, I do not see this as a moral or personal failing anymore. 

In my first year of studies I felt very lost. Everything was different - a new environment, new people, new circumstances, and new stusy methods. The goal was quickly moved from excelling to simply surviving this new set of changes. Needless to say, I was quite passive when it came to readings and interests outside of lectures. I focused the vast majority of my time on studying what was asked of me by the lectures. Back then I saw this as an *insufficiency* because in my mind a young scientist was supposed to absorb new information like an infinitely expanding sponge 24/7 and not get tired of it. Now, however, I am glad I approached my studies the way I did. 

Recently, this has been on my mind a lot and I have done some insightful reflections. I realized that back then, almost two years ago now, I was not sufficiently intellectually mature. By that I mean that I did not have a lot of experience studying independently. Despite excelling in highschool, I had to do a whole one-eighty in my studying approaches. It stopped being about regurgitating the information already presented by great teachers. Now I needed to set aside time to find books that would work for me, my background and approaches, I had to sit and read and struggle and try and fail and try again. My highschool experience and my university experience were like night and day. 

I remember feeling extremely lost and disappointed with myself and my "*insufficiencies*" (I used that word a lot when talking about myself). However, then I made a decision that now, looking back at, I feel very proud of. I decided to humbly sit down and start from zero. After very mid results in my first semester I realized that this is not the way to continue. Instead, when I did not understand something presented in the lectures, I would go back to my room and start reading not about that particular concept but the concepts before it. I started filling the gaps that had emerged in my knowledge that prevented me from fully understanding that "higher-level" topic or concept. And I would start from zero a lot. This was especially the case in my proof-based Linear Algebra course, the first mathematically rigorous and abstract course that I had taken. When the professor presented the vector field axioms in the first lecture, I was already lost but quitting was not an option. Instead, I started reading and re-reading the axioms, the basic theorems and the proofs that came with them. At first, it was all about parroting what I saw and read. Then, gradually, those concepts sedimented in my brain and I started actually recreating some basic proofs from memory and logical thinking. I also started tying those abstract mathematical concepts to my physics intuition. It was a slow and tedious process, at least an hour a day of reading and trying, but at the end my final grade in the course was 94%. 

I showed myself that hard work and persistent dedication actually yield results. I proved to myself that I was able to do something that just a few months ago seemed like an impossible task, and then I continued. I struggled through Analytical Mechanics, Electrodynamics, and other courses in this exact same way, though a little less each time. Reading, re-reading, doing, re-doing, again and again and again until it became as intuitive as 2+2=4. As a result, in my 4th (current) semester, I started feeling much more independent. I would still struggle through the new material in my courses but I would also get an hour or two a week of independent reading time which I slowly started using to my advantage. This semester, faced with courses like Quantum Mechanics, which are known to be difficult to most beginners, I struggled significantly less than ever before. My linear algebra knowledge and a good relationship with the Hamiltonian definitely helped, of course. I did not have to start from zero anymore. 

One of my peers asked me "why aren't you taking up additional courses? You could definitely do it." And it really got to me. I was wondering whether I was not doing enough. There is still so devastatingly much information that I do not know. Maybe I should try harder and spend more time studying... However, upon some further reflection, I realized that no. My approach is good. I might not be as fast as some other people blitzing through courses and mastering concepts beyond my current comprehension but this inevitably leads to burn-out. Moreover, at the end of the day, no one can learn the nearly infinite amount of information accessible to us in this world. 

I might be a bit of a "late bloomer" but I have plans to take up a couple of additional courses in my next year of studies and focus more on mathematics since I am definitely leaning more towards theoretical physics which is a mathematically rigorous discipline. For my personal development, allowing that space and time made me a stronger person and a more curious researcher. Instead of trying to tackle graduate-level textbooks by just diving in and struggling to understand the theory without the necessary pre-requisites, I instead choose to first acquire those pre-requisites. In my opinion, this is a much more productive way to study. I realized this after trying to read Marx's "Capital" as one of the first books in my early communist reading journey. I perservered through the first 50 pages and at the end I was not able to gain anything particularly useful for a baby-com. Then, I turned to readings that were more accessible for someone starting out with the theory and I certainly know more than "two yards of linen producing a coat is human labour." 

Now, when I see unfamiliar concepts in papers or textbooks, instead of continuing to read and hoping it will eventually click (and inevitably feeling like a failure), I try to identify what it is that is stopping me from understanding it and go read about that first. It is a much slower and, perhaps, more boring process, however, approaching new information with this sense of humility has helped me feel more certain in the knowledge I gain and in my abilities to learn independently. Maybe that time and space led to a slower initial velocity but it is now certainly gaining momentum.

# Math is Easy

At the risk of sounding like an arrogant nerd, I will go out and say this - Math is easy. Despite being a physics student, I still often hear the stereotypical phrase that mathematicians hear, something along the lines of "Ohh all that math, I could never, I hated math in highschool" or "I liked math before it became letters". This is an understandable sentiment. Many people have had unpleasant experiences in mathematics classrooms and I was not always as comfortable with learning math as I am now. However, I think that with enough desire and a little bit of time, everyone can learn mathematics to a pretty advanced levewl (yes, even the theoretical proof-based stuff). 

I was kind of bad at math in the primary school. I did not find the concepts of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division difficult to grasp but I really struggled with performing these operations on large numbers. It was not extremely terrible where it required intervention but I certainly was not "the smartest cookie in the class" when it came to mathematics. I think in primary school, my consciousness was kind of half-baked. Many things I did I could not quite explain or rationalize or even remember so yes. Large numbers were a struggle. I did not pay much attention to it, however, in the hopes that math in middle school will go beyond this unnecessarily complicated arithmetic. And it did. In 5th grade my math grades actually increased and math was much more exciting. 

I never particularly struggled with word problems and when I did, I had picked up the habit of drawing the problems, just like in physics. The shift of focus towards more fundamental and slightly more abstract concepts over arithmetic of 4-digit numbers was also better for me. I do not know what exactly allowed me to be good at math because, admittedly, I did not have to try very hard. It just came to me. I did all the homeworks, however, and I would always ask questions or go up to the board when given the opportunity. I think I really just approached it with curiosity and active engagement. Despite that, I could not avoid some questionable math classroom experiences. throughout the 4 years of middle school, I had the same female math teacher who was frankly a little sexist. She would always spend more time paying attention to how the boys were performing rather than the girls and so again soon I became more of a "mid-tier" student with 80% or higher on my tests but rarely 100%. 

This did not discourage me, however, and I soon started feeling like the pace of the classes was way too slow for my learning. My mom had taught me how to solve quadratic equations and do some other highschool algebra stuff when I was in the last year of middle school. She even expressed to my math teacher that I could get some additional problems because at that point most of the time I would finish the assigned classwork 15 minutes before the end of the lesson. My teacher did not agree with this philosophy and did not try to encourage me forward but I was doing better on the tests the more context I had behind the operations we were performing in class. 

I think up to this point in mathematical endeavors the biggest thing was that the teacher would spend most time with the students who were struggling and had special programs or the students who were exceptionally excelling. I was not exceptional so for the longest time I thought I was just mid at math. Turns out, I just really needed a little push which I luckily got during my highschool years. 

First year of highschool, I was a newbie at this school and I thought that everyone there was super smart. I was told not expect that my grades would stay up the way they did before so I did not. However, very soon I realized that the only tangible difference between this good highschool and my public middle school was the size of the classrooms and the motivation of the teachers. Back in middle school, there were easily 30 students in my class but in highschool there were only 18. The teachers also for the most part were willing to give you a challenge if you wanted one. I got the push that I needed to study even more and inquire on my own time about more math. Whenever I would encounter a topic I struggled with, I would conquer it by doing as many problems as I could and reading whatever I could get my hands on regarding the topic. 

My highschool math performance was quite impressive, even to me, my worst critic. And then I started university. I had already written about how the first year was quite hard for many reasons. One of them was the new structure. Instead of doing 20 short lines of algebra a day now we had 5 weekly complex tasks, each requiring somewhat intricate synthesis of the lecture and past knowledge. Then, I took a couple of theory-based courses and started appreciating more my main math professor whom at first I quite disliked. 

The more theoretics I read, the more I realized that math really isn't all that special. It is just a huge toolkit of methods that are built on theorems and lemmas which are built on some fundamental axioms (simple assumptions). Every time I struggled with understanding a theorem, I would first look at the definitions of the objects described in the theorems. Let us consider an example from Lebesgue integration, which I am currently self-studying. 

`**Lebesgue's Dominated Convergence Theorem**: Let (f_n) be a sequence of complex-valued measurable functions on a measure space (S, sigma, mu). Suppose that the sequence converges pointwise to a function f. Assume moreover that the sequence f_n is dominated by some integrable function g. Then f_n, f are integrable and {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\int _{S}f_{n}\,d\mu =\int _{S}\lim _{n\to \infty }f_{n}d\mu =\int _{S}f\,d\mu }.` [This is a somewhat condensed version of the theorem from Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominated_convergence_theorem)

Because this is a theorem a math major would be exposed to after taking at least 5 theoretical math courses, it contains a ton of definitions. Let us explain it in plain English. A **sequence** is just, well, a sequence of functions, in this case (just like 1, 2, 3, 4,... is a sequence). It will be sufficient to say here that it is an infinite list of items, in this case, functions. A **measurable function** is one that can be measured using a **measure** and a measure is nothing more than another function that just applied a nonnegative value to an object (and this helps mathematicians make conclusions about said object). A **measure space** is a special set of sets that has a measure. Now, it is said that the sequence **converges pointwise** to some value - function f. This means that as we go down this infinite list of functions f_n, we get closer and closer and closer to function f. The sequence is **dominated** by g just means that g acts as a kind of "ceiling" to our sequence. No element in the sequence will ever exceed the value of g. To be **integrable** means that the Lebesgue integral can be calculated for g and in turn for each f_n and f. Finally, we have the fancy formula that just says we can swap the location of the limit sign from outside the integrand to the inside. This theorem just gave us a tool to deal with limits in Lebesgue integration. 

Obviously, there a subtleties behind these definitions and the more you read and sit with them, the better you understand them but breaking these difficult theorems and notations into simpler terms is usually very beneficial in understanding what they really mean. When I was younger, I imagined that to study math one had to be a winner of olympiads and be a huge nerd who never got anything wrong. Now, I know that it is really a matter of patience - how long you are willing to sit staring at the theorem and rewriting it until it finally makes sense in your brain. I am sure once you go into grad or postgrad level of theoretics, it might be harder and the connections between simple definitions and their lemmas might not be as obvious but I cannot tell you this for sure as I haven't tried it. 

If you ever wanted to relearn math, I would highly suggest you pick up a pre-calculus textbook, then learn single-variable calculus and then try and dabble in some theoretics - read an analysis textbook. It will not be easy but it will be very rewarding and the more you do it, the easier and more rewarding it will get. Maybe saying that math is easy was a bit of an *oversimplification* but the spirit of the statement stays the same - patience and consistency - nothing more, nothing less!

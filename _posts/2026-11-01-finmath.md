# "Trust me, bro, the market will regulate itself"

I am currently taking a financial mathematics course. The most interesting part of it is the implementation of various mathematical models to real-world problems in Python. However, it also comes with a bit of very basic theory. Our professor is a researcher in mathematical physics (particularly, quantum mechanics), and from what I could deduce, does not participate much in the "stock market" personaly. Last week, I told him: "You know, I used to always joke about how non-sensical the finance world is and this course gives me insight into how non-sensical it really is." He could not disagree with me and said that finances is a purely human endeavour. It is not nature where we can have nice elegant laws and models that are almost guaranteed to give us satisfactory results. 

The models for option pricing, for example, make a lot of sense and are robust and elegant (if we lived in a perfect world with some awfully convenient simplifying assumptions). The mathematics is fascinating and exciting, the only poblem is - stochasticity. Stochasticity that finance bros usually ommit in their "tips for investing". The logic I have mostly heard is "if the graph goes up, it will most likely go up". Which is, of course, not something that we can predict. The first thing we were told in the course is that we cannot make deterministic statements and that nothing is really constant. Moreover, our simplifying assumptions, though do not completely disagree with reality, still ommit some interesting stuff about the market. For example, we have not considered inflation. And the professor told me that "inflation" is difficult to define and calculate and there are different inflations depending on the context and the country. 

We had a homework task that required to calculate the amount of money a person had to put in monthly into some bond paying 2% interest to get enough money for retirement. The answer was a little more than 700 dollars. The professor interpreted this as "you should be putting this much money into low-risk savings each month once you work". And that is all cool and all but bonds do not compete with inflation! Saving money with this amount of interest, your savings slowly devalue - you're losing money! Of course, putting the money into a low-risk asset still mitigates at least some of the devaluation but it still feels like an absolute scam. As a regular individual with finite funds, it is very unlikely you will win in the investment gamble. I used to be quite sceptical of this before but now that scepticism has increased even more. 

As someone who (unfortunately) needs to read about economics and try to decipher Marx's coat pricing, taking this course gives me an even more interesting insight into how unfair the system is. One of the common calculation assumptions is that the market is liquid - there is always money to be borrowed and lent. However, the amount of wealth accumulated in the hands of the few (I know, I am throwing hammers and sickles at you, sorry) could make it so that this is not the case anymore, at least from what I understand. Winning the investment gamble considerably can only be done if you have a lot of money to lose ("you gotta spend money to make money") and you put the money in many "different baskets". 

It is also even more absurd that leaders of countries talk about avoiding crises or implement things such as "print more money" (LITERALLY, by the way) to help the economy recover during a crisis. Marxists know that economic crises arise not from a deficit but from a surplus. It is a tragi-comedic reality of the system. When you have more supply than demand, the price* of a commodity decreases. Your commodity is worth less -> you make less profit from selling it -> you want to sell more -> you produce more. It is a very silly hamster wheel, the system just spiraling in on itself. This is not a bug but a feature. The market system works exactly as intended. I recall having an economics class in highschool. Our professor teacher told us "An economic crisis does not necessarily mean it is bad for *you*. You might be able to not get burned by its effects." And this is another thing. Some people are too wealthy to get affected, they might even be able to take advantage of the situation (say, the housing cost crisis). The ones who will almost surely get burned are the ones at the very bottom, the workers who have the least control over how things should be run. 

When I was having the after-lecture interaction with my professor, I caught myself before saying "oh, yes, this whole finmath stuff really puts my marxist readings into perspective". That would have most probably been a blunder. Despite interacting with a ubiquitously recognized author on this topic, I still need to be careful to not *appear ideological* (remember, kids, professors are not your friends and you should not be going around flaunting your "evil" politics around). I guess my biggest issue is not the math itself - the math is fine, the binomial trees are beautiful (I love Markov chains <3), it is more with the application and interpretation of it. The math does not consider the people in this financial world, it is only concerned with maximizing the shareholder value.
